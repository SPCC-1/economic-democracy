What you are leading

This is a structured public-problem inquiry for students in grades 9–12. Students investigate one AI use, document evidence and conversations, and contribute findings to a shared civic record.

Students are not expected to write New York City policy alone. You are not agreeing to purchase an AI product, teach prompting, or permit student use of generative AI.

Who can use it

School day

Civics, humanities, technology, entrepreneurship, career education, advisory, or interdisciplinary project-based learning.

Beyond the school day

Community schools, youth programs, libraries, summer programs, and community-based organizations.

What you need

  • Four 75–90 minute periods or program sessions.
  • One adult facilitator and a consistent student group.
  • The provided readers, rubric, and two-page handouts.
  • Two or more stakeholder voices beyond the classroom.
  • A way to submit the group’s Evidence Contribution Record.
  • A local audience that can hear, question, or respond to the work.

The assigned sources

Two student readers, two pages each.

Written for grades 8–12 and drawn from primary sources: the NYCPS guidance and the UNESCO student framework. Assign both before Session 1. Each is available as a web page and a printable PDF.

Reader 1 · The NYC rules What is allowed, what is paused, and who decides next? Two pages: the rules by grade, the five high-school pilots, what the city’s tool review does not check, and the April 2027 deadline. Reader 2 · What students need Are you a user of AI, or someone who can shape it? Two pages: what UNESCO says students should learn, and the difference between having access to a tool and having a say over it.

Two ways to run it

Inquiry Mode

Students study AI systems, policy, cases, and lived experience without using generative AI. This is the default.

Approved-Tool Mode

Where authorized, students may test a specific tool under a written protocol. Every activity still has a no-AI path.

Your first three moves

  1. Choose the student group and schedule. Start with the four-session core.
  2. Choose one specific use and two or more voices. Keep the inquiry small enough to document well.
  3. Plan a local demonstration. A classroom, school, CBO, library, or neighborhood audience is enough.

The Civic Lab grows through contributions, not attendance.

Groups submit evidence before they are invited to present. The Civic Lab team verifies and organizes contributions in a Community Learning Ledger, connects related findings, and prepares student groups for local and city-facing public demonstrations over time.
