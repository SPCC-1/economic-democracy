Section II · The Instrument

The civic instrument.

A structured public process — paced to the City's own rollout — through which a borough can deliberate the policy unfolding in it, and produce informed civic voice the administration can use.

What It Is

Most public participation in a major City decision falls into one of two categories. There is the town hall — open, immediate, often loud, rarely structured. And there is the policy comment period — structured, written, accessible only to people already inside the policy conversation. Neither produces what we are proposing: an informed public, working from shared material, arriving at a considered position over months, in public, alongside the policy as it unfolds.

The instrument has a familiar shape. It is built from elements already at work in New York City — classroom-based learning in schools and out-of-school programs, train-the-trainer models in CBOs and faith communities, civic competitions and public demonstrations of learning. None of it is new. What is new is applying that infrastructure to the economic-policy decisions the City is already making — turning what is now done for entrepreneurship or for arts education into a practice for democratic economic deliberation.

The City is going to make the decision either way. The question is whether it makes it alongside an informed public that has done the work to understand the choice — and that emerges from the process able to do the same work on the next decision, and the one after that.

How It Runs

A cycle in four phases.

A single borough-wide cycle runs over the course of a school year, paced to the policy unfolding inside it. The four phases below describe what a complete cycle looks like once the model is up and running.

I

Train the trainers

Months 1–2

The cycle begins with the adults. Teachers, youth-program leaders, CBO staff, faith-community organizers — anyone in the borough who works with young people or community members — go through the curriculum themselves, on the issue the borough is taking up. They learn it before they teach it.

This is not professional development for its own sake. It is the load-bearing step: the people who will lead small groups in their schools and community sites have to be able to teach the material honestly, hold all sides fairly, and know the local data. A 20-trainer cohort can serve hundreds of participants in the same cycle.

II

Run the lab

Months 3–5

Each trainer runs the Civic Lab with their group — in a classroom, an after-school program, a youth-development cohort, a congregation's adult-learning session, a community center. Multiple cohorts run in parallel across the borough. They are not synchronized, but they are working on the same material and converging on the same question.

Each cohort produces its own draft position — defended, evidence-based, internally debated. These drafts are the raw material for what comes next.

III

The public demonstration of learning

Month 6

The cohorts come together for a structured public event hosted by the Borough President's office or a comparable civic convener. This is not a debate or a competition. It is a deliberation. Cohorts present their positions; participants question each other; a facilitated process moves the room toward consensus where it exists and names disagreement honestly where it does not.

City representatives are in the room — not as judges, but as the audience the work is for. Press, the Borough President's office, community leaders, families. The event itself is the borough being seen thinking.

IV

The borough position paper

Months 6–8

The output is a written document. A short, public, synthesized statement of where the borough's informed participants landed — what they agreed on, what they disagreed on, what they want the City to know as the policy decision is made. Drafted by the trainers and the participants. Reviewed publicly. Delivered to the administration.

It is not binding. It does not need to be. It is what every City Council member, every commissioner, every reporter covering the policy can pick up and read in fifteen minutes to know what an informed slice of the affected community actually thinks — not what an advocacy group says, not what a poll captures, but what a process produced.

Who Is in It

The participants, named.

The instrument is only as strong as the structure of who runs it and who shows up. A borough-wide cycle composes from groups already in place across New York City — none of which has to be invented, several of which already work together.

Convener

The Borough President's office

Hosts the cycle. Brings civic standing, the convening power to pull the room together, and the legitimacy to deliver the position paper to the City. The natural civic seat for a process about a borough's economic future.

Curriculum partner

Economic Democracy / SPCC.1

Provides the curriculum, trains the trainers, coordinates the cycle, drafts the final position paper. The continuous thread across boroughs and across years. The civic-engagement partner the City contracts.

Trainers

Teachers and youth-program staff

The cohort that goes through the curriculum first and then runs it with their groups. Drawn from DYCD-funded out-of-school programs, public schools willing to run the lab as an elective or unit, and youth-development organizations already in the borough.

Participants

The cohorts who run the lab

Young people and adults from across the borough. Schools, after-school programs, youth-workforce cohorts, congregations' adult-learning groups, CBO-led community sessions. Many cohorts in parallel, working on the same material from different vantage points.

Faith communities

Churches, synagogues, mosques

Long-standing convening institutions in every borough, with adult cohorts and intergenerational reach that schools cannot replicate alone. Their leaders are trained as trainers; their members participate as a cohort.

Audience

The City and the press

The demonstration of learning is held in public, with City representatives in the room and the press invited. The borough is not asking to be heard. It is making itself visible as a thinking civic body — and giving the City and the press something honest to engage with.

Why It Is Different

This is not the room you know.

The public process the City already knows

Reactive. A decision is announced; people respond after the fact.

Unstructured. Whoever shows up. Whoever yells loudest. Whoever has the time.

Uninformed by design. No expectation that participants have studied the issue.

One-shot. An event happens, and it ends.

Adversarial. The City defends; the public attacks. Or the City listens politely; nothing changes.

What this instrument produces

Alongside. Runs in parallel with the policy timeline, not after it.

Structured. A curriculum, a cohort model, a facilitation method, a documented output.

Informed by design. Every participant has worked through the material before the room comes together.

Cyclical. The cohort that ran one cycle is ready to anchor the next. The model compounds.

Generative. The City and the public are working on the same problem — not opposing each other.

What the First Cycle Actually Looks Like

Start small. Prove it. Then scale.

What is described above is a full borough cycle running at scale. The first cycle is much smaller, and it should be. A first cycle in the Bronx, paced to the Hunts Point store opening in 2027, might run with one trained cohort of 12–15 teachers and program leaders, serving 150–300 participants across five or six sites, culminating in a single public demonstration at the Borough President's office and a first position paper delivered to the administration.

That is the proof of concept. From that first cycle, three things become visible: what works, what doesn't, and who else wants to join the next one. Year two scales the model. Year three travels it to the next borough as that borough's store comes online. Year four to the next.

The instrument is designed to be small enough to start with confidence, and structured enough that scaling is not improvisation. The next page describes what a 2-to-3 year partnership to build it looks like.
