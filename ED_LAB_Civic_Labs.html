The Application · The Third Strand

Civic Labs.
Real decisions. Real research.

The Framework shows students the argument. The Concepts give them the language. The Civic Labs are where they enter the argument themselves — by investigating a live economic decision in their own community and taking a position they can defend.

The Idea

Most economics is taught as settled fact. Real economic life is a sequence of contested decisions with no answer key. A Civic Lab hands students one such decision — a real one, unfolding now, in a real place — and asks them to do the work a citizen actually has to do: gather the data, talk to the people affected, state the strongest version of every side, and then decide. Not what to think. How to think — on a question that matters.

Section I · Live Now

The Flagship: Who Gets to Eat Affordably?

The first Civic Lab is built around a decision happening right now in New York City — its plan to open a publicly owned grocery store in Hunts Point, one of the poorest neighborhoods in the country. It is complete, classroom-ready, and the template every future lab follows.

● Live Civic Lab 01 · Food Affordability · Hunts Point, The Bronx
Civic Lab 01

Who Gets to Eat Affordably?

Should a city build and own a grocery store? Students investigate the real players, the real money, and the genuine disagreement — steelmanning the case for the store, the small-grocer's objection, and the alternative of helping the stores already there.

The lab includes: verified neighborhood data · three steelmanned positions · two background readers (the history and the economics) · five printable field tools · the full eight-beat investigation.

Section II · Expanding

More Labs, In Development.

The flagship proves the model. The next labs take on decisions that reach beyond any single city — the questions every community in the country is being asked to answer.

In development

The Cost of Growing Old

Social Security — the hardest long-term economic decision a society makes, and one almost no one understands. Students investigate how it works, why it's strained, and what the real tradeoffs of fixing it are.

Who pays, who benefits, and what do we owe each other across generations?
In development

The Machines Next Door

Data centers — the physical backbone of the AI economy — are being built across the country. Students compare how three real communities weigh the jobs, the power and water costs, the tax deals, and who actually benefits.

When a town trades its resources for growth, who gets the upside?
Section III · Anatomy

What's in Every Civic Lab.

Each lab is a complete, ready-to-run package built on the same structure — so once a teacher has run one, they can run any of them. The shared research tools carry across every lab; only the issue changes.

01 · The Framing

A real decision

An eight-beat investigation of a live, contested choice — with verified data, the players, and the genuine disagreement laid out fairly, every side steelmanned.

02 · The Readers

The background

Short companion readings that give students the history and economics they need to understand what is actually at stake before they take a position.

03 · The Field Tools

The real work

Printable, photocopier-ready instruments — interview guides, surveys, data sheets, a position worksheet — so students do genuine research, not worksheets.

Section IV · For Students

The lab they work in looks different — on purpose.

A teacher evaluates the curriculum on a page like this one: calm, complete, rigorous. But the surface a student actually works in is built differently — brighter, bolder, rooted in the place the lab is about. The shift in feel is deliberate: it marks the move from studying a decision to investigating one.
