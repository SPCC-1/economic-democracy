The one number that tells the truth

Net Worth

The one number that tells the truth about your wealth — not what you earn, but what you've actually kept.

01The concept

Net worth is the single number that captures your whole financial position: everything you own, minus everything you owe. It's the real scoreboard of wealth — not your salary, not your income, but what you've actually accumulated and get to keep. Assets minus liabilities, full stop.

Everything in the earlier groups feeds this one number. Your net worth is just the sum of all your equity — every slice you own across a home, a retirement account, a business, savings — added up, with your debts subtracted. If you want to know whether you're truly building wealth, this is the number to watch.

02How it works

You build the number the way an accountant would: total what you own, total what you owe, and take the difference.

That number can be positive or negative, and building wealth simply means growing it over time. There are only two ways to do that — and this is where the whole section comes together: grow what you own (offense), and shrink what you owe or protect it from being wiped out (defense). Net worth is the one scoreboard where both show up.

03In real life — income is not net worth

This is the trap that fools almost everyone. A paycheck and a net worth are completely different things:

High income, low net worth
A big salary spent as fast as it arrives — nice car, big rent, little owned, plenty owed. Looks wealthy. Isn't.
Modest income, high net worth
An ordinary paycheck, lived below means, owned assets for years. Quietly, genuinely wealthy.
Negative net worth
Student debt, few assets, a number below zero. Normal early in life — a starting line, not a verdict.

Income is what passes through your hands. Net worth is what stays.

04Apply it to your life
Find your number
  • Calculate it once: list what you own, list what you owe, subtract. Rough is fine.
  • Check it a few times a year. The trend matters far more than any single reading.
  • Name one move on each side: one asset to grow, one debt to shrink.
  • Be honest about whether a good income is actually turning into a rising net worth — they don't always go together.

What gets measured gets managed. Most people never calculate this number even once — doing it puts you ahead of the game.

05The honest part
What no one tells you

Net worth is the honest scoreboard, but read it honestly. It isn't a straight line — it dips in bad years when markets fall or a big expense hits, and a down year is not failure, though panic-selling in one can be. It can also be inflated by values you can't spend: a business "worth" a lot, home equity you'd have to sell or borrow against. A big number locked in illiquid assets is not the same as money you can use.

Two more truths. Starting at zero — or below — is the norm, not a personal failing: many people spend their first working decades at low or negative net worth because of student debt and wages that barely cover life, and that's structural. And the number is fragile: one uninsured catastrophe — a medical crisis, a disability, a lawsuit — can erase decades of building in a few months. That's why protecting your net worth matters every bit as much as growing it. The defense is coming up.

06The bigger picture
Why this matters beyond you

Net worth is how the wealth gap is actually measured — and it's far more extreme than the income gap people usually argue about. In the United States, the wealthiest 10% of households own roughly two-thirds of all wealth, while the bottom half holds only about 2 to 3%. Income is unequal; wealth is in a different league, because it compounds and concentrates across whole lifetimes and generations.

And net worth carries history in a way a paycheck doesn't — who was allowed to own, who could inherit, who was shut out — which is why gaps by race and family background are even starker in wealth than in earnings. So net worth is the truest single picture of economic democracy, or its absence: not what people earn, but what they own. Moving more households from zero or negative into real, positive, owned wealth is the entire project, written as one number.
